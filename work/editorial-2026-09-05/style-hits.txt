LaTeXBookCode/src/chapters/51_ch13.tex:21:\emph{No statistical engine.} A language model predicts the next token from patterns in its training text; it does not run least squares, invert a matrix, or compute a standard error. \textcite{bender2021parrots}, in the \enquote{stochastic parrots} critique, put the point sharply: the LLM captures the \emph{form} of language, not the meaning or the arithmetic behind it. It has read millions of regression tables and can produce something shaped like one, but asking it to \enquote{estimate} a coefficient in prose returns an imitation, not a calculation. The research on numerical tasks points the opposite way: accuracy rises sharply when the LLM is made to \emph{write code that a real interpreter executes} rather than compute in its own head. That is the finding behind program-aided language models\index{program-aided language models} \parencite{gao2023pal} and program-of-thoughts prompting \parencite{chen2023pot}, both of which delegate the computation to a Python interpreter and beat asking the LLM to reason numerically on its own.\marginnote{The machine-learning literature arrived at the section's thesis from its own direction: separate the reasoning (the LLM writes a program) from the computation (a real interpreter runs it). Stata is simply a better interpreter than Python for statistical work.} That is precisely the arrangement we recommend, with Stata as the interpreter: you do not have to coax a statistical layer out of the LLM, because the program and the data you already have supply one.
