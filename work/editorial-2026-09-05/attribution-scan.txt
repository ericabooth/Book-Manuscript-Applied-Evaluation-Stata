LaTeXBookCode/src/chapters/21_ch03.tex:11:If leaning on these sources feels like a shortcut, consider that empirical economists spent the past decade making the same move. \textcite{card2010administrative} pressed the quality argument early, urging their field toward records generated by the administration of taxes and programs because those records cover more people, more accurately, than any survey an evaluator could field; a few years later \textcite{einav2014bigdata} surveyed the shift toward administrative records with near-universal coverage and called it the defining data development of the era. Meanwhile the surveys were decaying. \textcite{meyer2015surveys} tracked the household surveys evaluators once treated as the default benchmark and documented rising nonresponse, imputation, and measurement error. For a nonprofit analyst the consequence is concrete: two files we work with later in this chapter, the Bureau of Labor Statistics wage file (QCEW) and the federal Common Core of Data enrollment file (CCD), are administrative censuses, not samples, and citing them puts your context numbers on the same footing as the published literature's.\marginnote{The turn did not stop at economics. A full volume of the Russell Sage Foundation's social science journal surveys administrative data across sociology, education, and criminology; there \textcite{penner2019administrative} make the case in terms an embedded evaluator will recognize: records generated by service delivery answer questions surveys cannot afford to, and they track the same people over time without re-contacting them.}
