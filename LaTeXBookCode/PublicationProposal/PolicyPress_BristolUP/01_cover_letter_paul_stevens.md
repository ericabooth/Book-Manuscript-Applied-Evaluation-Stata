# Cover letter — Policy Press / Bristol University Press

*To: `paul.stevens@bristol.ac.uk`. Works either as an opening enquiry (stop after paragraph five and offer the proposal) or as the covering email for the full package. Subject: Proposal enquiry: a practice-based quantitative methods book for evaluators*

---

Dear Paul,

Your subject page says you are working to expand Bristol's social research methods list beyond its qualitative and creative-methods strengths, and that you are particularly interested in books concerned with methodology and in skills- and practice-based books. We are writing about one that is squarely in that second category, and we would value your view on whether it fits before we take up more of your time.

The book is *Applied Program Evaluation Using Stata: A Practical Workflow from Data to Deliverables*. It is a complete 328-page manuscript, fifteen chapters and four appendices, and it teaches the whole job an embedded evaluator does: getting the data, defending the claim, and delivering the result to people who will act on it.

The gap it addresses is a practical one. Methods texts generally begin once the data is clean and stop once the model is estimated. Practising evaluators spend most of their week on either side of that: fourteen mismatched spreadsheets dropped in a shared drive, two administrative files describing the same people with no identifier in common, a programme director who will not read a regression table, and a funder who expects an interactive dashboard rather than a PDF. We wrote the book because we have done this work for years, one of us at a policy think tank and one at an evaluation consultancy, and because the analysts we train kept asking for something that covered the parts nobody writes down. We should say plainly that it is a personal project rather than institutional work: we contract as individuals and no employer holds rights in the manuscript or the code.

Three things may make it relevant to what you are building. First, it is genuinely practice-based rather than a text about method: every number printed in the book is produced by a companion do-file we can rerun on request, so a reader can reproduce the entire book. Second, it takes seriously the constraints your practitioner readers actually work under: locked-down IT environments, data-use agreements, small samples, and audiences who need the finding in a spreadsheet. Third, it includes what we believe is the first sustained treatment of using large language models inside an evaluation without losing reproducibility or leaking confidential data, alongside a chapter on preparing files for release without re-identifying the people in them.

We should be straightforward about one thing. Bristol has published very little that teaches statistical software; the nearest title we found was Cole Davis's *SPSS Step by Step* from 2013. We are proposing this to you rather than to a software publisher because the readership is yours, not theirs. The book is for the practitioner audience Policy Press already serves with titles like Helen Kara's *Research and Evaluation for Busy Students and Practitioners*. It simply gives that reader the code as well as the reasoning. If that is a direction you are not looking to go, we would rather know early.

We would classify it as a Learning Resource under your format categories. It has the pedagogical apparatus your textbook guidance asks about: worked examples, exercises at the end of every chapter, callout boxes stating what each statistical guarantee depends on, and substantial companion digital content in the form of runnable code, public datasets, and roughly two dozen Stata packages we wrote and released ourselves.

A full proposal following your guidelines is ready, along with CVs and sample chapters, and the complete manuscript is available whenever it would be useful. We are happy to start with a conversation instead if that suits you better.
