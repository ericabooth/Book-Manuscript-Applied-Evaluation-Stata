Dear Editors,

We would like to propose *Applied Program Evaluation Using Stata: A Practical Workflow from Data to Deliverables*, a complete 336-page manuscript in fifteen chapters and five appendices. The proposal form, our CVs, and four sample chapters are attached.

The book is about how public-policy evidence actually gets made. Governments, foundations, and nonprofits commission evaluations to decide whether programmes work, and the analysts who answer those questions spend most of their time on problems no methods textbook describes: a partner agency drops fourteen mismatched spreadsheets into a shared drive, two administrative files describe the same people with no identifier in common, and the finding has to reach a programme director who will not read a regression table and a funder who expects an interactive dashboard. Between those two ends sits the part textbooks do cover, estimating something defensible. We treat all three as one continuous piece of professional practice and teach it end to end.

That argument now has a name and a route through the book. Scott Long's *The Workflow of Data Analysis Using Stata* set the standard for discipline inside an analysis, and we extend that discipline into the parts of a project Long could take as given and an evaluation shop cannot: metadata that has to survive years of merges, column names that drift between vintages, extracts that arrive with undocumented surprises, and a reviewer who has to correct the record without touching the data. Chapters 1 and 2 set the route out, and Chapter 6 walks it end to end on one worked example: three yearly wage extracts arrive as a CSV file, an Excel file, and a Stata file, one of them quietly renaming a column between years, and the chapter works them through to an analysis-ready panel that answers "where did this number come from?" on its own. Appendix E runs the route again over thirty pages, carrying one public dataset from ingest to finished deliverable. Five companion Stata packages implement the route, all of them free and public, two already distributed through SSC. An analyst buys the book for that route: it answers the question in front of them this week and leaves them a pipeline they can rerun next quarter.

We are practitioners rather than methodologists. Eric works at a policy think tank, Elizabeth at an evaluation consultancy, and the workflow in this book is one we have built up across our careers. We also teach parts of it in workshops for policy doctoral students and early-career researchers, which is where the demand for it came from. To be clear about the position: this is our own book rather than institutional work, we contract as individuals, and no employer holds rights in the manuscript, the code, or the software packages.

Two chapters go where the literature has not. One sets out a framework for using large language models inside an evaluation without losing reproducibility or leaking confidential data, a problem every research organisation now has and almost none has written down. Another treats the disclosure-control question directly: how to release a dataset publicly without re-identifying the vulnerable people in it. Both are written for practitioners facing the problem this quarter.

We should be straightforward about how this sits against your list. Anthem does not currently publish research-methods or statistical-software books, and we are not proposing a software manual. This is a book about the craft of applied policy research, written for the people who do it, which teaches its methods in working code rather than in prose. The readership is the applied social science and public policy audience Anthem already publishes for, and the market is a large and poorly served one: analysts in government agencies, foundations, school districts, and health departments, plus the graduate programmes in public policy and public health that train them. We make that case in full in the proposal rather than pointing at comparable titles, because there is no comparable title.

The practical position is straightforward. The manuscript is finished and compiles clean. Every number, table, and figure printed in it is generated by a companion do-file we can rerun on request, so nothing in the book is unverified. There are no permissions to clear: all data used is public or simulated, and every figure is ours. We can deliver a final typescript within three months of contract.

One point of business before we go further. We are proposing this for the standard Anthem imprint, with no author contribution of any kind. Please confirm that, and send us the royalty schedule and a note on who pays for copyediting.

We have attached chapters 1, 2, 6, and 13, ninety-two pages and about a quarter of the book: 1 frames the role and previews the route, 2 builds the project scaffold, 6 is the capstone that carries the argument end to end, and 13 is the AI chapter. We would be glad to send the full manuscript, any other chapter, or the companion code whenever it would help.
