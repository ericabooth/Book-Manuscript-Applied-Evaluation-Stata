## One thing to know before you send

**Your materials, including your CV, are shared with reviewers unless you say otherwise.** Their page states this explicitly, and their review is *not* double-blind: they disclose author credentials to reviewers on purpose. If you would rather your CV were withheld, say so in the email; otherwise assume reviewers see everything.

## Two form fields need your decisions

1. **"Have you contacted other publishers?"** Answer honestly. If Stata Press, Vernon, or Policy Press has the proposal at the same time, say so. Presses expect simultaneous submission these days and being caught concealing it is worse than disclosing it.
2. **"Please recommend 5–10 peer reviewers."** They ask for names, affiliations, emails, and want you to flag which are personally known to you. **I left this blank on purpose**. I will not invent real people's contact details, and choosing reviewers is your call. `02_proposal_form_content.md` has a frame showing the five kinds of reviewer to name.

## What happens next, and when

- **Internal review** by the publishing team, screening for whether the book is "academically distinctive, is commercially viable and fits our publishing programme."
- **External peer review** of the proposal plus sample chapters: **3–4 weeks** from receipt. This is a real published number and it is fast.
- **Publishing committee** (editorial, production, sales, *and marketing*) makes the final call.
- If accepted, the acquisitions editor sends a contract. The full manuscript then goes out for a second review of roughly 8–12 weeks.
- **Contract to publication:** not stated anywhere on their site. They claim "a streamlined publication process faster than traditional presses" but attach no numbers, so treat that as marketing rather than a commitment.

## Cost, and the MONEY WARNING that matters most in this package

**Read this before you send anything.** The standard Anthem imprint costs an author nothing. Their FAQ describes the contract as "outlining the details of the agreement, such as delivery dates, manuscript requirements and remuneration," and there is no book processing charge, no subvention, and no open-access fee on that route.

**The risk sits next door.** Anthem runs a sister imprint, **First Hill Books**, on an author-funded hybrid model: in their own words, authors "support financially to offset some of the extra costs of their book's publication." Anthem does not publish what that support costs. Their open-access charges are not published either; both are quoted privately, one enquiry at a time. So a proposal sent to Anthem can come back as an offer from First Hill, or as a standard-imprint offer with an open-access charge attached, and you would meet the number for the first time in a reply email.

**Close that off in the first email.** The cover letter now carries a short paragraph that states plainly: we are proposing this for the standard Anthem imprint, with no author contribution of any kind; please send the royalty schedule; please say who pays for copyediting. Do not cut that paragraph to make the letter tidier. If any reply mentions First Hill Books, an author contribution, a subvention, or an open-access charge, get the figure in writing before you do another hour of work on the proposal.

**One more thing the money question cannot fix.** Anthem lists 38 subject areas and none of them is statistics or research methods, so nobody at the press can technically vet the Stata code. Their peer review will assess the argument and the writing, not whether the commands run. Plan for the code review to come from elsewhere: name a Stata user-community reviewer in the proposal's reviewer slots, and say in the email that you can supply the companion do-files and a reviewer who can run them.

## Honest read on fit, and read this before you spend an evening on it

You said you like Anthem, and on your stated criteria they earn it: free, independent, mid-size, low-friction, and a published 3–4 week turnaround that most university presses will not commit to in writing.

**The subject fit is the weakest of the four traditional presses, and it is worth going in clear-eyed.** I could not find a single Stata title, statistical-software title, or program-evaluation methods book in their catalogue, and none of the 38 subject areas they list is Statistics or Research Methods. The nearest thing is a 2020 book about how journalists use statistics, which is a different animal.

You now carry two risks and they are different in kind. The first is the imprint question above: read every reply for First Hill Books, an author contribution, or an open-access charge, and settle it in writing early. The second is fit: a committee with sales and marketing in the room looks at a software-teaching book with no comparable title on their list and cannot see who buys it.

**So the proposal is written to lead with the social science, not the software.** The letter positions the book as applied public-policy research practice that happens to teach its methods in code, and the "why publish" section argues the readership from scratch rather than pointing at a shelf they do not have. Both documents now name the route the book teaches and the five free packages that implement it, and both say in plain words what a practitioner gets for the money, because the sales and marketing seats on that committee decide on exactly that. If you want to improve the odds further, the single most useful thing you can add is concrete evidence of demand: workshop enrolment numbers, download counts for your packages, or a professional association that would promote it.
