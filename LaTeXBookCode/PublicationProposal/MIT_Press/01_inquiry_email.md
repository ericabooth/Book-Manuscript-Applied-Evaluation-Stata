Dear Ms. Woods,

We are writing to ask whether *Applied Program Evaluation Using Stata: A Practical Workflow from Data to Deliverables* is of interest to you. It is a finished manuscript of 336 pages, fifteen chapters and five appendices, and it teaches the whole job an embedded analyst does for a government agency, foundation, or nonprofit: getting the data, defending the claim, and delivering the result to people who will act on it.

The contribution is a single workflow, and it has a clear ancestor. Scott Long's *The Workflow of Data Analysis Using Stata* (Stata Press, 2009) taught a generation of analysts to organize their own files, write each decision down, and rerun a whole project from a master do-file. This book extends that discipline into the parts of a project Long could take as given and an evaluation shop cannot: metadata that has to survive years of merges, column names that drift between vintages, extracts that arrive with undocumented surprises, and a reviewer who must correct the record without touching the data. Chapter 6 walks the route end to end on one worked example, in which three yearly wage extracts arrive in three different formats and one of them silently renames a column between years. The chapter carries that mess to an analysis-ready panel that answers "where did this number come from?" on its own, and it includes the loop most pipelines skip: a reviewer reads the generated codebook, spots a wrong data vintage, corrects the metadata rather than the data, and the correction goes back in under an overwrite guard with a row-by-row receipt. Five companion Stata packages run that route, and all five are free and public. A 30-page appendix repeats the argument on one public dataset, from first download to finished deliverable, so a reader can rebuild every number.

The field is applied program evaluation and public policy research methods. The readers are the people who do that work for a living: analysts in state agencies, school districts, health departments, foundations, and nonprofits, together with the public policy and public health graduate programs that train them. They are a large audience and a poorly served one, because methods texts generally begin once the data is clean and stop once the model is estimated, and these readers spend most of the week on either side of that.

We should say plainly that we are practitioners rather than academics. Eric Booth works in applied evaluation and data systems at a policy think tank; Elizabeth Teas is a research scientist at an evaluation consulting firm. We teach parts of this material in workshops for policy doctoral students and early-career researchers, which is where the demand for the book came from, and we wrote and released the Stata packages it teaches. The book is our own project rather than institutional work: we contract as individuals, neither employer holds rights in the manuscript, the code, or the packages, and our personal contact details are below.

We also know where this sits on your list, or rather where it does not. MIT Press has not published a book that teaches Stata, and we are not proposing a software manual; the code is how this book teaches the practice. The nearest neighbor we could find is Martin Huber's *Causal Analysis*, the same length as our manuscript and priced at $60 in paperback, which suggests the format would translate. If a colleague of yours would read it better, we would welcome the referral, and if it is a direction MIT Press is not looking to take, we would rather hear that early than late.

Following your submission guidance we have attached nothing. A prospectus, a detailed table of contents, sample chapters, and our CVs are ready whenever you ask for them.

Thank you for considering it.
