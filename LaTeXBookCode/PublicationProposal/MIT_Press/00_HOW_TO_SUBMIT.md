# MIT Press — how to submit

**Route:** a short email inquiry to one named editor. There is no web form, no submissions inbox, and no proposal form to fill in.
**Send to:** **Catherine Woods**, Executive Editor, Economics, Finance and Business, `cawoods@mit.edu`
**Their page:** [Submitting a book proposal](https://mitpress.mit.edu/submitting-book-proposal/)

## This press works in two stages, and stage 1 is one email

MIT Press splits submission in two, and their instruction for the first stage is specific: an inquiry *"should simply describe, in a few paragraphs, the project, its authors or editors, its primary field, its intended readership, and the contribution it will make or need it will fill."* The full proposal comes later, and only *"if the editor is interested."*

`01_inquiry_email.md` is that email, written to their five points and ready to paste. Subject line:

> Book inquiry: *Applied Program Evaluation Using Stata* (complete manuscript, 336pp)

## Attach nothing

Their page says it plainly: *"Please do not send manuscripts, sample chapters, or illustrations unless an editor specifically requests them."*

So nothing goes with this email. No chapter, no table of contents, no CV, no PDF of the manuscript. This is the one package in this folder where the finished manuscript is a sentence rather than an attachment, and sending files unasked is the fastest way to look like someone who did not read the page.

## Write to one editor, and only one

Also explicit: *"Contact only one editor about your proposal; if the project would be best considered by another editor, your initial contact will refer your inquiry to that person."* Emailing a second editor to widen the net works against you here, because their own process already routes the inquiry.

**Catherine Woods is the right and only address.** She is Executive Editor for Economics, Finance and Business, takes on twenty to twenty-five projects a year, and works with *"an emphasis on high-impact trade books, professional business titles and field-defining textbooks."* That last category is the door this book goes through. MIT Press has no statistics editor and no data-science editor, so there is no better-matched colleague waiting to be found; Woods either takes it up, refers it, or declines, and all three answers come quickly.

## If she asks for a full proposal

Four things, per their page: a prospectus, a detailed table of contents, **two to four sample chapters** (*"ideally, about one-fourth of the work should be submitted"*), and an up-to-date CV. `02_if_invited_checklist.md` sets out how to assemble each one and where the text already exists in this folder.

The prospectus carries one demand the other four presses did not make. Their guidance on competing books asks you to *"be as frank as possible"* and to *"mention all pertinent titles."* That means naming Scott Long's *The Workflow of Data Analysis Using Stata* and arguing against it directly. Read the note in the checklist before you write that section, because Stata Press publishes Long and may hold this proposal at the same time.

## Peer review and the board calendar

- **Two rounds.** Their statement: *"Books published by the MIT Press generally undergo two rounds of peer review, one at the proposal stage and a second once the complete manuscript is drafted."* Because your manuscript is finished, the second gate arrives sooner than it would for most projects.
- **Textbooks may go to more reviewers than monographs.** If Woods positions this as a textbook, which is the likeliest route onto her list, expect a wider review panel and a longer first round.
- **The Editorial Board is MIT faculty and *"meets four times each year."*** The contract decision waits for a meeting, so the gap between an editor's yes and a signed agreement runs in months. That quarterly cycle is the only timing number MIT Press publishes; nothing on their site states an elapsed time from inquiry to decision.

## AI disclosure is a live obligation, so settle it before you write

Their policy: *"Authors who use AI tools to produce text or images/graphics, or to collect data, must inform their editors of this use and be transparent about it in their manuscripts."*

Two consequences. First, the duty runs to the editor, which means Woods should hear it from you rather than find it later. Second, Chapter 13 makes silence conspicuous: a book that teaches evaluators how to work with language models under guardrails will be read closely on how its own authors worked with them. Decide the wording once, then reuse it in the prospectus and in the manuscript's front matter. What it says is your call, and it is the one item in this package nobody can draft for you.

## Cost

None. No submission fee and no book processing charge. Their open-access program does not reach this book either: *"Trade Collection publications and textbooks are not available as a part of Direct to Open."* MIT Press publishes no royalty rates anywhere, so terms stay unknown until an offer arrives.

## Honest read on fit

**This is a longshot. Send it anyway, because it costs one hour and no attachments.**

The catalog is the fact that calibrates everything else. **Search the MIT Press catalog for "Stata" and you get five results, three of which are about the Frank Gehry building on the MIT campus.** The two real hits, Damian Clarke's *Applied Microeconometrics* and Frank Westhoff's *An Introduction to Econometrics*, use Stata to teach econometrics rather than teaching Stata. MIT Press has never published a Stata how-to book. There is no shelf here for this book to join.

One title does fit, and the email names it: Martin Huber's *Causal Analysis* (MIT Press, 2023) runs 336 pages, exactly the length of this manuscript, at 7x9 inches and $60 in paperback, and it was a 2024 PROSE Award finalist. It proves MIT will publish a methods book at this size and this price. It also shows the shape they publish: a methods book by a professor, written for academic use.

So the odds are what they look like. Weak subject fit, no comparable titles, an editor whose list is economics and business, and two authors who are practitioners rather than professors. Against that sits real prestige, one email, and a fast answer either way. Send it in the same week as the others and keep Stata Press and Vernon as the base case.

## What to do if she says yes

1. **Ask what she wants first.** Her request defines the package; do not send the whole thing on spec.
2. **Assemble the four required pieces** from `02_if_invited_checklist.md`. Most of the text already exists in this folder and needs cutting rather than writing.
3. **Write the AI disclosure** and put it in the prospectus, not in a later email.
4. **Tell her who else is holding it.** Presses pay real money for external review, and a press that learns about competition from you is fine.
5. **Name reviewers.** Their page invites suggestions and asks you to state your relationship to each person. That list is yours to make, as it is in every other package here.
6. **Ask when the Board next meets.** It sets your calendar, and asking signals you understand their process.
