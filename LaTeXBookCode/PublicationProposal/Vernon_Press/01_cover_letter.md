Dear Editors,

We would like to propose *Applied Program Evaluation Using Stata: A Practical Workflow from Data to Deliverables*, a complete 336-page manuscript with fifteen chapters and five appendices. The proposal form is attached, along with one sample chapter.

The book teaches the whole job an embedded analyst does for a nonprofit, foundation, or government agency: pulling data from public APIs and from agencies that publish nothing but a download button, linking administrative records that share no identifier, making a claim at the level of rigor the data supports, and delivering the result in a format the client can actually open. Most statistics texts start after the data is clean. This one starts before it arrives and keeps going after the model runs.

Your guidance says you look for work that "may draw from and unite existing approaches into a comprehensive or cohesive whole, resulting in a volume suitable for reference or student instruction." That describes what we set out to do. The individual techniques here are not new; what has not existed is one book that connects them, so a reader can follow a single project from an empty folder to a delivered portal without switching software or losing the thread. We wrote it because we needed it in our own work over many years, and because the analysts we train in workshops kept asking for it. It is our own project rather than an employer's: we write and contract as individuals, and no employer holds rights in the manuscript or the code.

Two things distinguish the manuscript from a conventional methods text. First, it ships working code: every number printed in the book comes from a companion do-file we can rerun on request, and the book teaches roughly two dozen Stata commands we wrote and released ourselves. Second, it covers ground the field has not written down yet, including a chapter on using large language models in an evaluation without losing reproducibility or leaking confidential data.

The sample chapter is Chapter 6, the argument in miniature. Three yearly wage extracts arrive in three different formats, one of them renames a column between years without saying so, and the chapter carries that to an analysis-ready panel that can answer where any number came from, including the step most pipelines skip: a reviewer reads the generated codebook, finds a wrong data vintage, and corrects the metadata rather than the data. This is the ground J. Scott Long's *The Workflow of Data Analysis Using Stata* could take as given and an evaluation shop cannot.

We note that your Statistics and Quantitative Methods list is a small one and that we could not find a competing title on it. We have made the case for the readership directly in field 6 rather than positioning against books you already publish. We would also point to *An Introduction to Computational Macroeconomics* as the closest structural precedent in your catalogue: a methods book taught through software, with companion files a reader downloads and runs.

The manuscript is finished. We are not asking you to wait on a draft, and we can send the full text, any single chapter, or the companion code the day you ask for it.

Thank you for considering it.
